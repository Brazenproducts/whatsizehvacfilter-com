What Size HVAC Filter Do I Need?

You're standing in the filter aisle or staring at your return vent wondering what goes there. This site exists to answer that one question clearly.

No quizzes, no account signups. Just straightforward steps to find your filter size, understand the numbers, and order the right one.

Three Steps to Your Filter Size

1

Check the existing filter

Pull out the filter currently in your system. The size is almost always printed on the cardboard frame — three numbers like 20x25x1 (width × height × depth in inches).

2

Measure if the print is gone

If the label is worn off or missing, measure the filter with a tape measure. Round to the nearest inch. The nominal size (what you order) is usually rounded up from the actual size.

3

Match and order

Use the nominal size to buy your replacement. A 19.5 × 24.5 × 0.75 inch filter is sold as 20×25×1. The slight gap is normal and necessary for the filter to slide in.

Nominal vs. actual — why the difference?

Filter manufacturers round up to clean numbers for ordering. The actual filter is slightly smaller so it fits into the slot without jamming. A 20×25×1 filter is never literally 20 inches wide. This is normal and not a defect.

Don't Know Where Your Filter Is?

Not everyone has a visible return vent on the wall. In some homes the filter lives inside the air handler, in a basement utility closet, or behind a ceiling grille you've never opened. Once You Know Your Size

Getting the right size is the hard part. Choosing a filter is simpler than the internet makes it seem. For most homes, a standard pleated filter in the MERV 8–13 range does the job without restricting airflow.

Frequently Asked Questions

Can I use a slightly different size filter?

No. A filter that's too small leaves gaps where unfiltered air bypasses the media. A filter that's too large won't fit and you'll damage the frame forcing it in. Get the exact nominal size.

What does MERV mean?

MERV stands for Minimum Efficiency Reporting Value. It rates how well a filter captures particles. Higher isn't always better — MERV 13 catches more but also restricts more airflow. Most homes do well with MERV 8 to 11.

Are expensive filters worth it?

It depends on what you're paying for. A well-made pleated filter at MERV 10 or 11 is a solid middle ground. The cheapest fiberglass panels barely filter anything, and the most expensive options are overkill for typical residential systems.

My filter size has four numbers — what's the fourth?

Some filters list the actual dimensions alongside the nominal size. The nominal size (the rounded-up one) is what you use to order. Ignore the actual measurements when shopping — they're just for reference.
